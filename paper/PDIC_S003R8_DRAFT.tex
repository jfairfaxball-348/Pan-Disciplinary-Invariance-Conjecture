\begin{abstract}
Let $U$ be an eight-point subset of $\F_3^3\setminus\{0\}$ with no nonempty zero-sum subset of size at most three. For a connected graph $G$ on eight vertices, uniformly label its vertices bijectively by $U$, and let $N_0$ count four-vertex \emph{maximal} independent sets whose labels sum to zero. We obtain the entire four-point distribution of $N_0$ from three graph-incidence statistics. In particular, $\PP(N_0=3)\leq1/105$, with equality if and only if $G$ is the spider tree with leg lengths $(2,2,2,1)$. The theorem, including the all-cap classification bridge and the actual $8!=40{,}320$ labellings, is formalised and kernel-verified in Lean 4 at a pinned source commit. The cap geometry is historically classical; mathematical priority of the combined distributional result remains under review.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and main result}
The intersection pattern of the zero-sum four-subsets of a small finite-field cap can govern a graph statistic that ordinary maximal-independent-set counts do not capture. The probability space here consists of \emph{all} bijections from the eight graph vertices onto a \emph{fixed} admissible cap; we do not choose maximal independent sets uniformly. Throughout, graphs are finite, simple and undirected, and \emph{maximal} means inclusion-maximal, not necessarily largest cardinality.

Write $\cF(G)$ for the set of four-vertex maximal independent sets. Define $m=|\cF(G)|$, let $n_t$ count \emph{unordered} distinct pairs in $\cF(G)$ meeting in $t$ vertices ($t=0,2$), and let $h$ count \emph{unordered} triples $\{S,T,R\}\subset\cF(G)$ having a disjoint (therefore complementary) pair $S,T$ with $|R\cap S|=|R\cap T|=2$. The disjoint pair is unique within such a triple. Set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moments}
 M_1=\frac{2m}{35},\qquad M_2=\frac{2n_0}{35}+\frac{n_2}{315},\qquad M_3=\frac{h}{315}.
\end{equation}
\begin{theorem}[Complete quartet-witness law and sharp extremiser]\label{thm:main}
For every connected simple graph $G$ on eight vertices and every fixed admissible eight-point cap $U\subset\F_3^3\setminus\{0\}$, let $\lambda:V(G)\to U$ be uniform among the $8!$ bijections and put
\[
 N_0=\#\left\{S\in\cF(G):\sum_{v\in S}\lambda(v)=0\right\}.
\]
Then $N_0\in\{0,1,2,3\}$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:law}
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\PP(N_0=0)&=1-M_1+M_2-M_3,\\
\PP(N_0=1)&=M_1-2M_2+3M_3,\\
\PP(N_0=2)&=M_2-3M_3,\\
\PP(N_0=3)&=M_3=\frac{h}{315}\leq\frac1{105}.
\end{aligned}}
\end{equation}
Equality in the last inequality holds if and only if $G\cong S(2,2,2,1)$, the eight-vertex tree with one four-valent centre, three arms of length two and one arm of length one.
\end{theorem}

\section{The cap and its four quartets}\label{sec:cap}
A set $U\subset\F_3^3\setminus\{0\}$ of size eight is \emph{admissible} if no nonempty subset of at most three distinct points sums to zero. Appending $0$ gives a nine-cap in $AG(3,3)$: in characteristic three, three distinct collinear points have sum zero, and a line through $0$ would contain an opposite pair. We acknowledge the classical affine classification of nine-caps, discussed by Follett--Kalail--McMahon--Pelland--Won~\cite[Proposition~2.2]{Follett2014}. The linear pointed normal form needed for zero-sum sums also has a complete finite Lean proof in the verified source discussed below; the classification is not an external axiom in the final formal theorem.

\begin{lemma}[Four-quartet incidence]\label{lem:quartets}
Every admissible $U$ has exactly four zero-sum four-subsets, say
\[
 Q_{11}=A_1\cup B_1,\quad Q_{12}=A_1\cup B_2,\quad
 Q_{21}=A_2\cup B_1,\quad Q_{22}=A_2\cup B_2,
\]
where $A_1,A_2,B_1,B_2$ partition $U$ into four two-point atoms. Opposite quartets $Q_{11},Q_{22}$ and $Q_{12},Q_{21}$ are disjoint and complementary; adjacent quartets meet in two points. Every triple of distinct quartets has one disjoint pair.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Use the pointed canonical cap $W_0\setminus\{0\}$, where
$W_0=\{(x,y,x^2+y^2):x,y\in\F_3\}$. It is a cap because $d_1^2+d_2^2\ne0$ for $d\ne0$ in $\F_3^2$; an affine line's three projected points $a-d,a,a+d$ have heights whose sum is $2(d_1^2+d_2^2)\ne0$. Translations along the paraboloid, $(x,z)\mapsto(x+t,z+2\langle x,t\rangle+\langle t,t\rangle)$, transitively permute its nine points. Thus an affine equivalence between a nine-cap and $W_0$ can be adjusted to fix zero, when it is linear, hence preserves sums.

The eight nonzero canonical points comprise four axis projections with height $1$ and four corner projections with height $2$. A zero quartet uses exactly two of each, by the height condition $2(4-a)+a\equiv0\pmod3$. Its projected sums vanish precisely when both chosen pairs are opposites. There are two opposite axis pairs $A_1,A_2$ and two opposite corner pairs $B_1,B_2$, giving exactly $A_i\cup B_j$ and the stated intersections. The complete finite pointed-orbit classification formalises the equivalence for every admissible cap, not just this canonical calculation.
\end{proof}

\section{The compatible-triple graph invariant}\label{sec:graph}
For any connected eight-vertex $G$, define $h(G)$ as above. The existence of one compatible triple forces a balanced bipartition: its disjoint four-MIS $S,T$ are both independent and cover $V(G)$. Connectedness makes this bipartition unique up to swapping sides. Conversely, every connected balanced bipartite graph has its two parts as maximal independent sets (there are no isolated vertices). Thus $h(G)$ is exactly the number of mixed maximal independent four-sets selecting two vertices from each part. In particular, it is zero if no balanced bipartition exists.

For the balanced bipartition $A\sqcup B$ ($|A|=|B|=4$), write $N(X)\subseteq B$ for the neighbours of $X\subseteq A$.
\begin{lemma}[Mixed-set criterion]\label{lem:mixed}
For $|X|=|Y|=2$, $X\subseteq A$, $Y\subseteq B$, the set $X\cup Y$ is a maximal independent set if and only if
\[
 N(X)=B\setminus Y,\qquad N(Y)=A\setminus X.
\]
In particular a valid left pair $X$ has the \emph{unique} right partner $Y=B\setminus N(X)$, and $|N(X)|=2$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Independence forbids edges between $X$ and $Y$. Inclusion-maximality requires every outside vertex to have a neighbour inside the set. These are precisely the two displayed equalities.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Sharp compatible-triple bound]\label{prop:sharp}
For every connected eight-vertex graph $G$, one has $h(G)\le3$, with $h(G)=3$ precisely when $G\cong S(2,2,2,1)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
It suffices to consider a connected balanced bipartite graph $A\sqcup B$, since otherwise $h=0$. Call an $A$-vertex \emph{low} if its degree is at most two; let $\ell$ be their number. By Lemma~\ref{lem:mixed}, every member of a valid left pair is low. If $\ell\le2$, there is at most one mixed witness.

If $\ell=3$, at most $\binom32=3$ pairs are possible. Suppose all three are valid. If one low vertex has two neighbours, each other low vertex must share only those two neighbours, since pairing it with the first has neighbourhood size two. But then the low vertex outside that pair has no neighbour in its right partner $Y$, contradicting maximality. Thus the three low vertices are leaves, with distinct neighbours in $B$ (otherwise one pair has only one neighbour). Connectedness then requires the sole remaining $A$-vertex to meet all four $B$-vertices. This is the spider.

If $\ell=4$, each $A$-degree is at most two. Connectedness entails at least seven edges, so there are either seven or eight edges. In the seven-edge case the degrees on $A$ are $(2,2,2,1)$. Replace each degree-two $A$-vertex by an edge joining its two neighbours in $B$. These three edges form a connected graph on four $B$-vertices, hence a tree (no loops or parallel edges). A valid left pair must contain the unique $A$-leaf, since two degree-two vertices would have to correspond to parallel edges. If its neighbour in $B$ is $b_0$, the other chosen $A$-vertex corresponds to a tree edge incident to $b_0$. There are at most $\deg(b_0)\le3$ choices. Equality forces the three-edge tree to be a star centred at $b_0$, again yielding $S(2,2,2,1)$.

In the eight-edge case all four $A$-degrees equal two and become four edges of a connected multigraph on four $B$-vertices. A valid pair corresponds to a parallel-edge pair. Connectivity prevents two distinct such pairs, since their four edges would cover at most two components without enough edges to join four vertices. A triple parallel class is likewise impossible. Thus there is at most one valid pair. Finally, for the spider the three pairs of distinct $t_i$ give the three mixed witnesses shown in Figure~\ref{fig:mixed}; hence $h=3$.
\end{proof}

\section{Incidence probabilities and the complete law}\label{sec:law}
Because $\lambda$ is a uniform bijection, the image of a fixed four-set is uniform over all $\binom84=70$ cap four-sets. Lemma~\ref{lem:quartets} therefore gives probability $4/70=2/35$ for one zero quartet. Of all ordered configurations of two complementary four-subsets, four out of 70 are quartet pairs, again giving $2/35$. For two distinct four-sets intersecting in two vertices there are $\binom84\binom42^2=2520$ equally probable ordered image pairs, of which eight are adjacent ordered zero-quartet pairs; the probability is $1/315$. Other intersection sizes cannot occur between distinct zero quartets.

For a specified compatible triple $S,T,R$ with $S\cap T=\varnothing$ and $|S\cap R|=|T\cap R|=2$, the ordered image-triple configurations also number $70\binom42^2=2520$, eight of which are zero-quartet triples. Thus its joint zero probability is $1/315$. Each image configuration arises from exactly $(2!)^4=16$ bijections, because its four incidence atoms have size two; these are probabilities for the actual 40,320 equiprobable labellings, not an idealised surrogate space.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
Write $Z_S$ for the indicator that $S\in\cF(G)$ maps to a zero quartet, so $N_0=\sum_{S\in\cF(G)}Z_S$. The incidence counts give
\[
\EE\binom{N_0}{1}=\frac{2m}{35}=M_1,\quad
\EE\binom{N_0}{2}=\frac{2n_0}{35}+\frac{n_2}{315}=M_2,\quad
\EE\binom{N_0}{3}=\frac{h}{315}=M_3.
\]
If all four quartets were independent in $G$, no edge could run between the union of the two $A$-atoms and the union of the two $B$-atoms, as every cross-atom vertex pair lies in some quartet. These two nonempty vertex blocks would disconnect $G$. Therefore $N_0\le3$. For $P_i=\PP(N_0=i)$, the three binomial moments are $P_1+2P_2+3P_3$, $P_2+3P_3$, $P_3$, together with $P_0+P_1+P_2+P_3=1$. Solving yields \eqref{eq:law}. Proposition~\ref{prop:sharp} makes $h\le3$, with equality only for the named spider, as required.
\end{proof}
