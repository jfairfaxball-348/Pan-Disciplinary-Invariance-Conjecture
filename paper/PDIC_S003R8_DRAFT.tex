\begin{abstract}
Fix an eight-point subset $U\subset\F_3^3\setminus\{0\}$ having no zero-sum subset of size one, two, or three. Given any connected simple graph $G$ on eight vertices, uniformly label its vertices bijectively by the points of $U$. Let $N_0$ count those \emph{inclusion-maximal} independent sets of size four whose labels add to zero. We determine the complete distribution of $N_0$ from three small intersection statistics of the graph's size-four maximal independent sets. The key geometric input is a four-quartet incidence design, and the extremal graph is a single eight-vertex tree: $\PP(N_0=3)\le 1/105$, with equality precisely for the spider with arm lengths $(2,2,2,1)$. The fixed-cap, all-connected-graph, $8!=40{,}320$-labelling statement has a registered, independently kernel-checked Lean formalisation. This illustrated account distinguishes the classical cap classification, the elementary moment calculation, and the special graph bound; originality relative to the full literature remains unresolved.
\end{abstract}

\section{A finite-geometry experiment on a graph}\label{sec:intro}
The experiment involves \emph{two fixed objects} and \emph{one random choice}. First fix a particular admissible cap $U$ in $\F_3^3$, and a connected graph $G$ of order eight. Then uniformly choose a bijection $\lambda:V(G)\to U$. We do \emph{not} randomly choose the cap, a graph, or a maximal independent set. This distinction is crucial: all $40{,}320$ bijections have equal probability, while different independent sets may have very different dependencies.

A set of vertices is \emph{independent} if no edge has both ends in it. It is \emph{maximal} if no further vertex may be added while retaining independence; it need not be of maximum size. Write $\cF(G)$ for the \emph{four-vertex} inclusion-maximal independent sets (four-MIS). The random quantity of interest is
\[
 N_0(\lambda)=\#\bigl\{S\in\cF(G): \sum_{v\in S}\lambda(v)=0\in\F_3^3\bigr\}.
\]
Figure~\ref{fig:experiment} shows the objects and the only source of randomness.

For $t=0,2$, let $n_t$ count unordered distinct pairs $\{S,T\}$ in $\cF(G)$ with $|S\cap T|=t$; put $m=|\cF(G)|$. A \emph{compatible triple} is an unordered triple $\{S,T,R\}$ of four-MIS with one disjoint pair $S,T$ and $|R\cap S|=|R\cap T|=2$. That disjoint pair is unique. Let $h=h(G)$ count compatible triples and set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moments}
 M_1=\frac{2m}{35},\qquad M_2=\frac{2n_0}{35}+\frac{n_2}{315},\qquad M_3=\frac{h}{315}.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Complete witness-count law and sharp equality]\label{thm:main}
For every connected simple eight-vertex graph $G$ and every fixed admissible eight-point cap $U\subset\F_3^3\setminus\{0\}$, under uniform bijections $\lambda:V(G)\to U$ the above $N_0$ belongs to $\{0,1,2,3\}$ and satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:law}
\begin{aligned}
\PP(N_0=0)&=1-M_1+M_2-M_3, & \PP(N_0=1)&=M_1-2M_2+3M_3,\\
\PP(N_0=2)&=M_2-3M_3, & \PP(N_0=3)&=M_3=\frac{h}{315}\le\frac1{105}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Equality holds in the final bound \emph{if and only if} $G$ is isomorphic to the spider $S(2,2,2,1)$, the eight-vertex tree with three length-two arms and one length-one arm.
\end{theorem}

The mathematical story has three steps: identify the four zero-sum quartets of $U$ (Section~\ref{sec:cap}); control complementary and mixed four-MIS in $G$ (Section~\ref{sec:graph}); count coincident events among actual bijections and invert three binomial moments (Section~\ref{sec:law}). Figures~\ref{fig:cap}--\ref{fig:rare} are constructions or exact data illustrations, never substitutes for these arguments.

\section{Why the cap has exactly four zero-sum quartets}\label{sec:cap}
A cap here is \emph{admissible} if it contains eight nonzero vectors and no nonempty subset of one, two, or three distinct vectors sums to zero. Adjoining the origin gives a nine-point cap in the affine space $AG(3,3)$. The classical classification of these nine-caps implies affine equivalence to a quadratic-graph model \cite[Proposition~2.2]{Follett2014}; to preserve \emph{four-term} zero sums, the affine map must then be adjusted to fix the origin, making it linear. The verified formalisation proves the required pointed normal-form bridge internally.

One representative is $W_0\setminus\{0\}$, where
\[
 W_0=\{(x,y,x^2+y^2):x,y\in\F_3\}.
\]
For projected coordinate pairs $(x,y)$, the four axis points have third coordinate $1$, and the four corners have third coordinate $2$; $(0,0)$ has height $0$. In each class, opposite projected points sum to $(0,0)$. Figure~\ref{fig:cap} is a \emph{coordinate table}, not an affine embedding of the eight vectors in the plane.

\begin{lemma}[Quartet incidence]\label{lem:quartets}
For every admissible $U$, there is a partition into four two-point atoms $A_1,A_2,B_1,B_2$ such that its \emph{only} zero-sum four-subsets are
\[
 Q_{11}=A_1\cup B_1,\quad Q_{12}=A_1\cup B_2,\quad Q_{21}=A_2\cup B_1,\quad Q_{22}=A_2\cup B_2.
\]
Their distinct-pair intersections comprise exactly two disjoint complementary pairs and four pairs meeting in two points. Every three distinct quartets contain exactly one disjoint pair.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Work first in the displayed canonical cap. If a four-set uses $a$ axis points (height $1$) and $4-a$ corners (height $2$), its height sum is $a+2(4-a)\equiv 2-a\pmod3$. Thus $a=2$. The projected coordinate sum vanishes if and only if the two axis points are opposites and the two corner points are opposites. Each class has exactly two opposite pairs, so there are precisely $2\times2=4$ zero quartets, with the stated atom decomposition. The classical nine-cap equivalence, followed by a canonical affine automorphism taking the image of the origin back to the origin, yields a linear transformation. It preserves all sums and transports the entire quartet structure to arbitrary $U$.
\end{proof}

\section{How a spider controls compatible triples}\label{sec:graph}
A compatible triple necessarily contains disjoint four-MIS $S,T$, hence $S\sqcup T=V(G)$. Since these two independent sets form a bipartition of a connected graph, they are its \emph{unique} bipartition up to swapping. Conversely, for a connected balanced bipartite graph, the two sides are maximal (there are no isolated vertices). Thus $h$ equals the number of mixed four-MIS that choose two vertices from each side; for graphs without a balanced bipartition, $h=0$.

Fix bipartition $A\sqcup B$ with $|A|=|B|=4$. For $X\subseteq A$, denote the vertices adjacent to some $x\in X$ by $N(X)\subseteq B$.
\begin{lemma}[Two-by-two maximality test]\label{lem:test}
For $|X|=|Y|=2$ with $X\subseteq A$ and $Y\subseteq B$, the set $X\cup Y$ is a maximal independent set if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:test}N(X)=B\setminus Y,\qquad N(Y)=A\setminus X.\end{equation}
Consequently, an admissible left pair $X$ has a \emph{unique} partner $Y$ and every vertex of $X$ has degree at most two.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Independence means no edges link $X$ to $Y$. Maximality means that each vertex outside $X\cup Y$ has a neighbour in it. Those requirements are exactly the two equalities.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Sharp graph bound]\label{prop:sharp}
For every connected eight-vertex simple graph $G$, $h(G)\le3$, with equality exactly for $G\cong S(2,2,2,1)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Only balanced bipartite connected graphs require consideration. Fix sides $A,B$ as above and let $\ell$ be the number of $A$-vertices of degree at most two. By Lemma~\ref{lem:test}, each mixed four-MIS determines a unique pair among those $\ell$ vertices.

If $\ell\le2$, there is at most one. If $\ell=3$, at most $\bin{3}{2}=3$ are possible. To attain all three, the low vertices must be leaves: should one have two neighbours, the two other low vertices would have all their neighbours in the same two-point set; then for the mixed set using the first low vertex and one of the others, the omitted low vertex would lack a neighbour in the opposite-side selected pair, contradicting maximality. The three leaves must have different neighbours, or some pair has neighbourhood of size one. Connectedness then forces the fourth $A$-vertex to meet all four $B$-vertices, yielding the spider.

If $\ell=4$, connectedness implies at least seven edges and the total degree from $A$ is at most eight. With seven edges the $A$ degrees are $(2,2,2,1)$. Replace each degree-two $A$-vertex by an edge joining its two $B$-neighbours. The resulting connected three-edge graph on four $B$-vertices is a tree. A valid pair must contain the $A$-leaf: two degree-two $A$-vertices cannot have equal neighbour pairs in a tree. The other pair member corresponds to a tree edge incident to the $A$-leaf's neighbour $b_0$, so there are at most $\deg(b_0)\le3$ choices. Equality forces a three-edge star centred at $b_0$, giving the same spider. With eight edges all four $A$-degrees equal two, and the resulting connected four-edge multigraph on $B$ has at most one parallel pair: two parallel pairs or a triple class leave too few edges to connect four vertices. Only parallel pairs can be valid, so $h\le1$. The spider achieves three by Figure~\ref{fig:witnesses}.
\end{proof}

\section{Counting joint events and inverting moments}\label{sec:law}
Write $Z_S=\mathbf1\{\sum_{v\in S}\lambda(v)=0\}$ for $S\in\cF(G)$. A fixed four-set maps uniformly to each of the $\bin84=70$ label four-sets, of which exactly four are zero quartets. Thus $\PP(Z_S=1)=4/70=2/35$.

For distinct four-sets, only intersection sizes zero and two can give a joint event. For a disjoint pair their images are complementary; exactly four possible images for the first member are zero quartets, giving joint probability $2/35$. For a pair intersecting in two vertices, there are $70\bin42^2=2520$ possible ordered image configurations, eight of which are adjacent ordered quartet pairs, giving $1/315$. Three simultaneous zero events require a compatible triple; there are similarly eight favourable ordered incidence triples among $2520$ possibilities, again $1/315$. Every configuration of this type is realised by exactly $(2!)^4=16$ vertex bijections: the four two-point atoms can be permuted internally. This calculation refers to the actual sampling space, not a surrogate choice of random independent sets.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
Linearity of expectation over one-, two-, and three-subfamilies gives
\[
 \EE\bin{N_0}{1}=M_1,\qquad\EE\bin{N_0}{2}=M_2,\qquad\EE\bin{N_0}{3}=M_3.
\]
If all four zero quartets were independent in $G$, then no edge could join a vertex in $A_1\cup A_2$ to one in $B_1\cup B_2$: every such vertex pair lies together in one quartet. This contradicts connectedness. Hence $N_0\le3$. Writing $p_k=\PP(N_0=k)$ gives $M_1=p_1+2p_2+3p_3$, $M_2=p_2+3p_3$, and $M_3=p_3$, with $\sum_{k=0}^3p_k=1$. Solving yields all four expressions in \eqref{eq:law}. Proposition~\ref{prop:sharp} gives $h\le3$, with equality uniquely at the spider, proving the final claim.
\end{proof}
